# AI and Copyright: Who Owns the Work When a Machine Learns From It

Draft — 2026-10-31, personal rewrite

## This is not a threat paper

Copyright is not an attacker breaking in. No flaw, no key, no stolen password.

It is a law fight. Who owns work when a machine reads it and writes something new?

That matters to me because my house runs on one rule: work must be yours. If AI helped, say so. Learn from work. Do not lift work.

## The case

The New York Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft. Training on news articles — fair use, or copying at scale?

The Justice Department backed OpenAI in September 2026. Their argument: training transforms articles into new material, and AI matters for national security.

House Judiciary Democrats filed in Manhattan in October 2026, siding with publishers. Their argument: the 1976 Copyright Act has no national-security exception. If that exception works, copyright is gone for AI training, in every case.

DOJ declined further comment in that report. OpenAI did not respond. Microsoft declined comment.

I am not the court. I am not giving a verdict here. I am laying out the fight, because the fight is the point.

## France, same pattern

Meta lost appeals in France over press publishers' neighbouring rights. Old payment deal expired in 2024. No payment since 2025.

The authority ordered Meta to talk in good faith, share revenue and content-use data, and not degrade press content on screen.

The court said Meta's payment math may be abuse of a dominant position. Not a final finding. An interim order, while the merits get examined.

Two countries. One pattern. Publishers say: pay for the work, show your math. Platforms say: scale and importance change the rules.

## The other side, fairly

I will steelman it, or this paper is not honest.

Training is transformative. A model does not reprint articles. It learns patterns and makes new text.

If every training use needs a license, only giants can train. A small builder cannot license the whole internet.

AI matters for security, science, medicine, school. Slowing it with lawsuits hands the lead to rivals who do not follow our rules.

Publishers already get search and traffic. AI answers are a new kind of summary, not a replacement.

Neighbouring rights are a tax on linking, not a fair price. The math is secret, and secret math is not fair, no matter who hides it.

Those points are real. They just prove too much with no limit. Transformative use still hits the author's market. Small-builder access still needs a line that does not let a giant take everything free. National security still needs proof, limits, and an end date.

## My line

Pay. Credit. Show your work.

Learn from it. Rebuild it in your own words. Do not copy it verbatim. Do not sell a rebuild as original.

That is my build ethos, and my copyright ethos. Same line, both ways. If I use someone's work to learn, I credit it. If a company uses my work, or a newspaper's work, to train a model that competes with the original, I want permission, payment, or a fair-use line that actually holds.

## Questions I still have

If training is fair use, what is left of the author's market when the model answers in their style, on their facts, for free?

If training is not fair use, how does a small builder train anything without a lawyer?

Who shows the math?

Does a national-security claim need proof and an end date, or does it work forever?

What happens to a family builder, a student, a small publisher, when the rule is written for giants?

## Closing

Copyright is not a threat level. It is a house rule for a shared library.

If anyone can take anything if they call it AI and important, the library empties. Only giants can afford to write in it.

If you pay, you credit, you show your work, then builders like me can learn, rebuild, and still own what we make.

Learn from the work. Credit the work. Do not take the work and call it yours.
